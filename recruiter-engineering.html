Why ordinary matching misses

The pile is getting
harder to trust.

Good candidates can look wrong. OSHA 10, OSHA 30, journeyman licenses, NCCER certifications, PE licenses: every candidate spells their qualifications slightly differently. A keyword filter treats "OSHA 30 certified" and "OSHA-30" as different things, or misses a licensed structural engineer entirely because the resume says "PE" instead of "licensed." The strongest site managers often have the least polished resumes, with scale of work buried in a project name rather than spelled out for a filter to catch.

Weak candidates can look right. A resume can list every certification in the posting and still not tell you whether this person has run a live site, made a call under schedule pressure, or just been present for someone else's project.

The first pass needs more than a match score.

How it works

Three reads.
Then a person decides.

Every resume gets read three times, from three different angles, before anyone writes a shortlist.

Strict read

Does the candidate meet what the req explicitly asks for?

Scored against the spec as written. Finds relevant experience even when the vocabulary doesn't match, a resume that says "ran a $12M project under AIA A201" still matches a spec asking for "multi-million dollar contract experience." But absence of evidence still counts as absence. Unforgiving on purpose.

Transferable read

Could they do the work, even if the vocabulary is different?

Looks for evidence the person could do the job even when certifications are listed under an unfamiliar abbreviation, or the career path doesn't follow the obvious script. Adjacent contract forms, comparable project scale, work that reads as equivalent once you look past the label.

Hiring-manager read

Worth a conversation, and what would need testing?

One interview slot this week. Would you spend it here, and what's the specific thing you'd want to find out on the call?

The three reads are evidence, not a decision. A person goes through all of it, writes the ranked shortlist, and tells you where the reads disagreed and why. The software reads. Your recruiter decides.

When the keywords miss

An automation engineer's strict read landed low: fourteen years and not one day at a machine builder, and the posting's OEM requirement appeared nowhere on the resume. "On the words alone this is a straightforward reject." The transferable read caught what the strict read couldn't: fourteen years of debugging other people's lines under production pressure, two full line upgrades built from scratch, and food-sector experience the posting explicitly wanted. The hiring-manager read rated him a 9, first interview if the client could be persuaded.

When the keywords mislead

One application named every platform, every sector, every term in the posting: the highest surface match of forty-nine applications by a wide margin. The transferable and hiring-manager reads found little underneath it: eleven years across four roles without a single machine named, project value given, or commissioning location stated. "Highest keyword match in the pile, weakest evidence behind it. Any tool scoring on overlap ranks this one first." Kept on the shortlist rather than cut, ranked at the bottom, in case the missing detail was a client-confidentiality issue rather than the candidate's fault.

Disagreement is useful: when the three reads point in different directions, we don't hide that inside a single score. The reasoning stays visible so your recruiter can make the call, and the ones where the reads split are exactly the ones worth a second look.
